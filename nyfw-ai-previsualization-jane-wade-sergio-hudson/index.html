Fashion & Technology / Spring 2027

Backstage at New York, the rehearsal went digital

By The Splendid Edit September 2026 New York
← Back to The Edit

Two designers let Google into the sample room this month. The clothes stayed theirs. What the software touched came earlier, in the hours when a show is still a set of guesses.

Jane Wade and Sergio Hudson tested separate beta tools during New York Fashion Week, working with Google Envisioning Studio through Flow, the company's creative studio. Vogue Business reported the trials on 14 September, midway through the Spring 2027 shows. Neither designer handed over the collection. Each pointed the software at a specific bottleneck that arrives before anyone sees a runway.

The season ran 10 to 15 September on the Council of Fashion Designers of America calendar. Wade closed with a physical show of thirty-six looks. Hudson built a set and walked models through it in real light. The AI never appeared on the schedule. It sat behind the schedule, in the planning that the audience does not attend.

Wade

Wade used a styling tool to compare garments before fittings began. Colorways, fabrics, combinations, all rendered side by side. The output worked like a dressing card drawn in advance, a way to see how separate pieces might resolve into a single look without pinning anything to a body first.

Then the bodies arrived. Wade told Vogue Business that every runway look was still fitted on a person. She drew a second line around the campaign and editorial images, keeping those with the photographers, stylists and models who make them. The software narrowed the field. People made the calls.

That boundary matters more than it sounds. The thirty-six looks that walked were documented on Vogue Runway as clothes, made and fitted and sent out. The tool sped up the comparing. It did not sign the collection. Read that way, the experiment is smaller and more honest than the headlines it could have produced.

Hudson

Hudson tested the other end of the problem, the room itself. His tool modeled venue dimensions, lighting, mood, props and the flow of bodies through the space, with budget figures held alongside. The show could be examined before a single truss went up.

A runway lasts a few minutes. Its cost accrues over weeks of set changes, lighting tests and last calls that land too late to fix. A digital rehearsal surfaces the conflicts while they are still cheap. Move a look earlier in the sequence. Shift a light. Cut a prop that reads as clutter on the render before it reads as clutter in the room.

What the render cannot do is tell Hudson how the space feels when the seats fill. It cannot certify how a coat moves or how a model takes a turn at the end of the runway. It gives the producer, the lighting team and the communications lead one shared picture to argue over before the money is spent. That is the whole value, and it is a real one.

The line

Google has said little about what comes next. No general release, no pricing, no account of how the uploaded material is stored or trained on, no audited figure for what the trials saved. Those blanks sit next to a fact that is not blank: two designers ran the tools in genuine production and told a reporter where they stopped using them.

The stopping points are the interesting part. Unreleased collections, patterns, fitting images, venue plans and show budgets all carry value that a competitor would want. Before any of that enters a model, a house needs plain answers about retention, access and deletion. Wade's choice to keep the final imagery human is one workable rule. Silence is not a rule at all.

Fashion has spent two years bracing for the synthetic campaign, the collection with no maker, the image that fools the front row. This was the quieter version, and probably the durable one. The tools that last will be the ones that help people compare, rehearse and agree, then get out of the way while the clothes get made. New York, this season, drew that line in public. The next houses to try will decide where they draw their own.
