The Stay · Iguassu Falls

The house that keeps the falls after dark

By The Splendid Edit July 2026 6 min read
← Back to The Edit

The park closes at six and the buses pull out. The falls keep going. Inside Iguassu National Park, one pink house stays behind with them.

Iguassu is a wall of water on the border of Brazil and Argentina, close to three hundred cascades strung across two miles of rainforest. By day the walkways are full. Coaches arrive from Foz do Iguacu, the crowds move along the rails, and the noise of people almost competes with the noise of the river. Then the gates shut and everyone leaves. Almost everyone.

Hotel das Cataratas sits alone inside the national park, the only hotel on the Brazilian side of the falls. Guests who stay the night are handed something no day visitor can buy. They walk the paths before the park opens and after it closes, when the mist hangs over an empty gorge and the Garganta do Diabo, the Devil's Throat, thunders to nobody.

The house

A long pink palace in the Portuguese colonial manner, terracotta roofs, arched loggias, a white clock tower rising over the treetops. It opened on 4 October 1958 with fifty-two rooms, built when a hotel this deep in the forest was a national statement about Brazilian tourism. The jungle has grown around it since. From the air it reads as a single graceful line laid across the green, the falls a white curtain behind.

There are 187 rooms and suites now, most in soft creams and dark local wood, the best of them looking straight down the garden toward the water. Orient-Express, now Belmond, took over in 2007 and restored the building without arguing with it. Belmond sits inside LVMH today, and the property keeps a two-country rhythm: Brazilian warmth, European polish, the falls doing the rest.

After dark

On the nights around a full moon the falls throw a moonbow. Light off the moon catches the spray and bends into a pale arch that hangs over the gorge in the dark. It is a rare thing to see anywhere, and at Iguassu it belongs almost entirely to the people staying in the pink house, since the park is closed to everyone else by the time it appears. A guide walks guests out to the rails after dinner. The river runs silver. The bow holds in the mist.

The falls carry an older story too. Guarani legend tells of Naipi, promised to a serpent god, and Taroba, the warrior who tried to carry her away by canoe. The god split the river in his fury and dropped the lovers into the falling water, and the cataracts are what he left behind. The hotel bar takes Taroba's name. The legend is painted into the walk long before you reach the rail.

The table

Y Restaurant runs from an open terrace with the falls in view, the room where dinner drifts into evening and the sound of the water carries up the lawn. Ipe keeps things lighter by the pool, Brazilian and unhurried, built for the middle of a hot afternoon. Bar Taroba turns dark and close after dinner, cachaca and Brazilian spirits, the last stop before the walk to the moonbow.

The spa leans on Guarani ritual, treatments drawn from the plants of the forest around it. The hotel has earned EarthCheck recognition for the way it runs inside a protected park, where every choice is watched by the rainforest it borrows. There is a pool, a tennis court, a kids club for the young, bicycles for the paths. The World's 50 Best Hotels named it among its hundred finest in 2025.

The verdict

Most of Iguassu is a day trip. You arrive with the coaches, you leave with them, you see the falls at full volume and full crowd. The pink house is the way around that. Stay the night and the park becomes, for a few hours at each end of the day, almost private.

Book a room facing the water. Take the moonbow walk if the calendar allows it. Let the last bus go without you.

Hotel das Cataratas, A Belmond Hotel, sits inside Iguassu National Park on the Brazilian side of the falls. Rates vary by season; the moonbow walks run on the nights around each full moon, weather permitting. Book through belmond.com.
