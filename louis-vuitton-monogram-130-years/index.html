The Monogram, at one hundred and thirty

By The Splendid Edit 4 August 2026 Fashion
← Back to The Edit

A canvas drawn in 1896 turns one hundred and thirty this year. Louis Vuitton has given the whole of 2026 to it, and the celebration keeps circling back to where the pattern began.

The Monogram is the most copied print in fashion and the one Louis Vuitton guards most closely. Both facts come from the same source. Georges Vuitton drew the quatrefoils, the flowers and the interlocked initials to stop the forgers who had already started imitating the house. The signature was a defense first. It became a language after.

One hundred and thirty years later the house is spending a full calendar on it. The anniversary programme opened in January and runs through the year, with window displays, in-store presentations and a run of collections that treat the canvas as a subject rather than a backdrop.

The mark

Georges made the pattern in 1896 as a tribute to his father Louis, who had founded the Maison four decades earlier. He built it from the visual language around him. Neo-Gothic ornament supplied the geometry. Japonism, then moving through Paris in prints and lacquer and ceramics, supplied the flatness and the flower.

The result was a print that read as a maker's mark and a decoration at once. It went onto trunks first, then onto the softer bags that followed as travel changed. Speedy, Keepall, Noe, Alma and Neverfull each carried it into a different decade. The canvas outlasted every one of the uses it was designed for.

The flatness was practical. Coated onto cotton, the pattern sat on a surface that shrugged off rain and rough handling, exactly what a trunk crossing a continent needed. Form and decoration arrived together, which is why the print has never looked applied. It reads as the material itself.

Asnieres

The pattern has an address. The Vuitton family home and workshop sit at Asnieres, on the edge of Paris, where the house still keeps its historic atelier. Georges grew up there, and the story the house tells traces part of the Monogram back to the decorative tiles in the family kitchen and the Neo-Gothic detail of the period.

The anniversary makes the link literal. One of the new pastel shades in the season's revived canvas is named Vert Asnieres, a soft green that points straight at the house. It is a small thing, a colour name on a swatch. It also tells you where Louis Vuitton wants the eye to travel this year.

The capsules

Three collections carry the year. Monogram Origine returns to the 1896 canvas and reweaves it, trading the coated cotton for a linen and cotton jacquard in pastels pulled from the archive. The pattern softens. It reads as cloth rather than as logo, which is the point of going back to the start.

VVN takes a different route. It works in natural cowhide, a material the house has used since its founding, left to darken and mark with handling. The leather arrives pale and earns its colour from whoever carries it. Time Trunk plays the opposite game, printing the fittings and straps of historic trunks in trompe-l'oeil so a flat bag wears the memory of a solid one.

Each capsule argues for the craft behind the logo rather than the logo alone. Origine leans on the weavers, VVN on the tanners, Time Trunk on the layetiers who still build trunks by hand at Asnieres. It is a considered answer to a decade of noisy collaborations. The house is showing its hands.

The Monogram Origine capsule reweaves the 1896 canvas in linen and cotton jacquard. Courtesy of Louis Vuitton

The faces

The campaign gathers a cast that spans generations and markets. Catherine Deneuve sits beside Zendaya; HoYeon Jung and Liu Yifei carry the reach into Seoul and Shanghai. The pairing is the message. A mark this old has to prove it still belongs to the young without losing the people who grew up with it.

Pharrell Williams opened the year on the men's side, staging the anniversary within his own vocabulary for the house. The women's bags anchor the retail story. Speedy and Neverfull lead the January push, the two shapes most people picture when they picture the print at all.

The Monogram has survived reinvention before, from the Murakami cherry blossoms to the Supreme boxes to the current archive mining. At one hundred and thirty the pattern needs no rescue. The year reads as a long reminder of whose mark it is, and of how far a defensive gesture can travel once the world decides it wants a copy.
