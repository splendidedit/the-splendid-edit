The Season / New York

Loro Piana counts the microns

By Margaux Delacroix 23 November 2026 Fashion

Loro Piana, Cashmere of the Year Award, the Brant Foundation, New York. Courtesy of Loro Piana.

← Back to The Edit

On the tenth of November, Loro Piana filled the Brant Foundation with cashmere and the people who grow it. The house gave its eleventh Cashmere of the Year Award to three couples from Inner Mongolia. The winning fibre measured 12.89 microns.

The room

The Brant Foundation sits in the East Village, in a 1920s building that had been a power substation and then an artist's studio. For one night it held cashmere. Loro Piana took the ground floor and set the fibre out across surfaces, under low light, in a room arranged to be touched.

Upstairs ran an installation the house called Master of Cashmere: The Quest for Fiber Excellence. It traced where the material comes from and how Loro Piana reads it, through the herding, the sorting and the research that has gone into measuring a single hair. The displays explained the thing before the evening celebrated it.

The count

The award began in 2015. Each edition weighs the fibre that herders bring in and names the finest. Fineness here is literal. It is the diameter of one hair, read in microns, and the lower the number the rarer and the softer the yield.

First prize went to Mr. Siqingbilige and Mrs. Hasiqiqige, whose bale weighed 135.8 kilograms and measured 12.89 microns. Second went to Mr. Jiyatu and Mrs. Gegentuya, at 87.1 kilograms and 13.01 microns. Third went to Mr. Lifuqiang and Mrs. Qiqi, 116 kilograms at 13.04 microns. All three couples work in Inner Mongolia, and all were honoured for what they clipped in 2025.

Each winner took home a payment scaled to the weight of the clip, and a silver comb. The comb is the tool herders use to gather the underfleece by hand in spring. Loro Piana had it cast in silver, which turns a working object into a trophy without pretending it is anything else.

Eleven editions in, the award has become a fixture of the Loro Piana calendar. It began as a way to pay back the herders whose clip held up under the mill's testing. It has grown into the house's clearest public account of where its cashmere starts, and who draws it from the animal.

The fibre

Cashmere comes from the goat's underlayer, the soft down beneath the coarse outer hair that carries the animal through a continental winter. A goat yields a few hundred grams of it a year. The finest grades grow on the cold plateaus of Inner Mongolia and the steppe around them, where the swing in temperature pushes the animal to grow a denser, thinner down.

Loro Piana has bought from these regions for decades and has tied its name to the fineness of what it buys. The house works with baby cashmere, taken from kids at their first combing, where the fibre is finer still. The award sits on top of that supply chain as a thank-you and a standard at once. It tells the herders what the mill wants, and it pays more for the ones who reach it.

Micron by micron, the numbers have fallen since 2015. Loro Piana reads that as proof the arrangement works. Better fibre earns more, so the herding bends toward it.

“

The prize is a diameter. A hair thinner than any you could feel, grown on a plateau in winter and read in a downtown gallery to the hundredth of a micron.

Margaux Delacroix

The table

Dinner followed in a room done in dark wood and low light, with flowers set close on the tables. The three winning bales sat at the centre, raw and unspun, while the guests ate around them. Jeremy Strong came, and Bruce Springsteen with Jessica Springsteen. Jeff and Justine Koons, Tom Sachs, John Currin and Rachel Feinstein, Nicky Hilton, Huma Abedin, Indre Rockefeller and Gucci Westman filled out the room.

It was a New York crowd for a Mongolian harvest, which is the point Loro Piana keeps making. The herders rarely appear in the cities that buy the sweaters. For one evening the fibre carried their names, and the people who wear it had to look.

The century

Loro Piana passed its hundredth year in 2024. It spent most of that century as a wool and cashmere merchant before it became a name on a label, and it still presents itself through the raw material rather than a runway. Frederic Arnault runs it now as chief executive, and the award fits the house he leads: quiet, technical, built around a number.

LVMH owns Loro Piana, and the group has spent the past year staging its houses inside cultural rooms, from the Frick on Fifth Avenue to the Arts Decoratifs in Paris. The Brant evening is a smaller version of the same instinct. It borrows an art foundation for a night and fills it with the story of a material. The clothes were not on the walls. The fibre was.

The award will run again next year, and the microns will be read again. A luxury house can sell softness and never explain it. Loro Piana prefers to count it out loud.
