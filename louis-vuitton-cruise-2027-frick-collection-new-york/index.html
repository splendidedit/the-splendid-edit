The Season / New York

Louis Vuitton moves into the Frick

By The Splendid Edit 12 November 2026 Fashion

Louis Vuitton Cruise 2027, shown at the Frick Collection, New York. Courtesy of Louis Vuitton.

← Back to The Edit

Louis Vuitton showed its Cruise 2027 collection inside the Frick Collection in May, the first fashion show the New York museum had ever held. Then it stayed. The house signed on as the Frick's principal cultural sponsor for three years, and the collection's campaign reached the public this month.

The room

The Frick sits on Fifth Avenue, facing Central Park. Henry Clay Frick built it as a house and filled it with paintings, and it has been open to the public since 1935. The collection runs from the Renaissance to the end of the nineteenth century, with three Vermeers and a run of Rembrandts among the holdings.

The building reopened last year after a long renovation. Annabelle Selldorf drew it, and it opened the Frick's second floor to the public for the first time, along with a suite of rooms built for shows that come and go. That new space is part of what Louis Vuitton is now paying to fill.

Nicolas Ghesquiere set the runway through the glass-roofed Garden Court. Palms and Easter lilies ringed the central fountain, the columns and the coffered ceiling stayed in place, and the room was lit in deep orange. Cruise collections fall between the main seasons, and Louis Vuitton has shown them inside landmark buildings before, from a John Lautner house in Palm Springs to a Niemeyer museum in Brazil. The Frick traded that modernism for rooms hung with Old Masters.

A museum of this rank does not usually hand over its galleries. The Frick had its reasons. A long renovation leaves an institution with a larger operating bill, and a three-year sponsor helps carry it. The show put the clothes where the paintings live, and the paintings stayed on the walls.

The clothes

The theme was New York. Ghesquiere called it downtown attitude meeting uptown glamour, and the clothes carried both at once. The first look paired a denim outfit with a wide zebra belt. Overalls came rolled down and knotted at the waist over trompe-l'oeil trousers.

The cut stayed sharp. Collars stood up, shoulders lifted, and the line ran sculptural through the jackets and coats. Frilled collars answered the Elizabethan ruffs in the portraits on the walls. A boxing motif surfaced through the show, in gloves slung over shoulders, laced booties on the feet and satin shorts worn under tailoring.

Eveningwear closed the distance between the two moods. Column dresses in iridescent pink caught the orange light off the stone, and the palette ran back to a flat pop red. Bomber jackets turned formal over the boxing shorts. Cate Blanchett, Zendaya and Anna Wintour watched from gilded chairs set among the sculpture.

Haring

Keith Haring ran through the collection. His figures, the barking dogs and crawling babies he first chalked in the New York subway, printed across dresses, tops and bags as what the house called living canvases. Haring drew in the city in the early eighties and died in 1990, and his foundation licenses the work now. One look carried a leather suitcase from the thirties that he had marked up in 1980, and a miniature of it turned up later among bags shaped like takeout boxes and vinyl records.

The lease

The sponsorship is the larger move. Louis Vuitton is now the Frick's principal cultural sponsor for three years, and the lead backer of the museum's next three special exhibitions. The first, Siena: The Art of Bronze, 1450 to 1500, opened in October. A show on the French enameller Suzanne de Court follows, then a monographic display of nineteenth-century painting.

The money reaches past the galleries. Louis Vuitton First Fridays opens the museum free on the first Friday of most months, from June through next May. The house is also funding a two-year curatorial post, held by Yifu Liu, whose research covers the exchange between European and Chinese art in the eighteenth century. This is patronage with a payroll, not a one-night rental.

The stakes

American museums have leaned on corporate money for decades. What has shifted is the kind of company signing the cheque. A luxury house now funds the scholarship, the free days and the exhibitions, and stages its own collection in the same rooms. The Frick gains a steady line in its budget, and Louis Vuitton gains a Gilded Age address.

Bernard Arnault's group has built this muscle for years. The Fondation Louis Vuitton draws crowds to the Bois de Boulogne, Dior and Tiffany lend their names to shows at the Arts Decoratifs, and the group underwrites restorations across France. The Frick extends the pattern to an American institution. It puts one house at the centre of the arrangement rather than the group.

The campaign landed this month and the collection is in stores. By then the partnership had already outgrown the clothes. The show was the opening night. The three years are the point.
